The project that taught me the most about technology-enabled communication is one where most of the talking is done by software. 311.ai is a multi-agent system I built for city repair requests. A resident calls to report a pothole or a broken streetlight, and the system has to turn that call into an assigned repair crew. An earlier version won Best Use of TokenRouter at the UC Berkeley AI Hackathon in June 2026, where it took a resident's call to an assigned crew in under 60 seconds. In September 2026 I rebuilt it from scratch: a coordinator agent and three specialist agents sharing 19 tools, with Amazon Nova Sonic handling voice intake.

I started out treating it as an engineering problem. It turned out to be mostly a communication problem, in three places.

The first is the caller. People reporting a problem to the city don't speak in neat form fields. They describe a location by a landmark, mention two problems at once, or call about something a neighbor already reported. A voice agent that asks follow-up questions like a database prompt makes people hang up, so the intake has to sound like a person who is listening and still collect a usable address and issue type. The system also uses Redis vector deduplication, so when several people call about the same flooded intersection, the city sees one issue with several reports instead of a pile of separate tickets. That is a messaging decision as much as a technical one. The people reading the queue need a clear picture, not noise.

The second is the people who have to trust the system's decisions. When the agents choose a repair crew, they score the options and log the reasons in plain language. A dispatcher who disagrees can read why that crew was picked and overrule it. Without that, an automated decision becomes a black box that people learn to ignore. When a request involves safety or a budget call, the system doesn't decide at all; it escalates to a human. Working out what a machine should say, and when it should hand the conversation to a person, changed how I think about building anything people rely on.

The third is explaining the work itself. A system with multiple agents, a voice layer and a dedup layer is easy to describe in a way nobody follows. The version that lands starts with the resident's call and ends with the crew that shows up, and saves the architecture for questions. I applied the same thinking to CharlieTourGuy, a voice AI tour guide built with ElevenLabs and Google 3D Maps that placed second for Best Voice Agent at The AI Champion Ship in February 2026. For a tour guide, the product is the way it talks. Pacing and tone matter more than one more feature.

None of this happened in a vacuum. I'm a Mathematics-Computer Science student at UC San Diego with a 4.00 GPA, and during the school year I also work as a research assistant at Stanford, building a FastAPI backend for a real-time EEG stress-detection system, and as a software and research intern at UC Berkeley's RadWatch, where I maintain a Python pipeline that handles more than 10,000 sensor readings per second. Balancing that with classes and side projects means I can't count on long, open afternoons. What works for me is a short written plan every morning. Next to my computer is a stack of those daily write-ups, each one noting what I want to improve that day. It is a simple habit, but it keeps research deadlines, coursework and builds like 311.ai from crowding each other out.

Those commitments have shaped where I want to go. I want to build AI products that real people talk to: systems that understand messy human input, explain their decisions in words a non-engineer can check, and know when to step back. The technical side matters, but the part that decides whether anyone uses the product is communication. This scholarship would help me keep investing time in projects like 311.ai, where I can keep getting better at both.
